## I. Geographic Determinism and Physical Power Constraints

**Framework Name:** The Rimland-Heartland Duality (Kaplan / Mackinder / Spykman)
**Core Idea:** Global power is determined by the struggle between land-based powers controlling the Eurasian "Heartland" (the interior lowlands/tablelands) and sea-based powers controlling the "Rimland" (the maritime-oriented marginal zones). Control of the Rimland is the key to world power because it facilitates contact with the outside world and checks the expansion of the Heartland power.
**Node Applicability:** Strategic Competition and Regional Pivots. Directly applies to Russia (Heartland), China (Heartland-Rimland hybrid), and the "Debatable Ground" (Iran, Afghanistan, Turkey).
**Key Analytical Question:** Is the signal indicating an effort by a Heartland power to secure a warm-water port or break through the Rimland's encircling ring of states?
**Connection Implication:** Weight signals from Rimland nodes (e.g., Vietnam, South Korea, India) higher when they indicate shifts in maritime alliances that obstruct Heartland expansion.
**Red Team Challenge:** Technology (missiles, satellites, and air power) has significantly collapsed time and distance, potentially rendering the "Crisis of Room" more critical than static mountain-and-plain topography.

**Framework Name:** The Imperative of Place (Friedman / Stratfor)
**Core Idea:** A nation's character and foreign policy are dictated by geography, which imposes specific capabilities and vulnerabilities regardless of a leader's intent. Nations act primarily out of a "fear of catastrophe" rooted in their physical position (e.g., lack of natural barriers or dependency on distant resources).
**Node Applicability:** Kinetic Conflict and Regional Pivots. Relevant to "Borderlands" like Poland, Ukraine, or Kashmir, where overlapping national identities meet lack of natural defenses.
**Key Analytical Question:** What "menu of choices" does this specific topography leave the state, and which choice represents the path of least resistance to survival?
**Connection Implication:** Prioritize the relationship between a node's physical vulnerability and its propensity for "preemptive" or "aggressive" posture.
**Red Team Challenge:** Human agency and engineering (e.g., Grand Canals, transcontinental railroads) can fundamentally alter a geography's strategic value, turning "wastelands" into connective hubs.

**Framework Name:** The Asian Mediterranean (Kaplan / Spykman)
**Core Idea:** The South China Sea functions as a global "Asian Mediterranean," a congested maritime crossroads linking the Indian and Pacific Oceans through critical chokepoints like the Strait of Malacca. Control of this "middle sea" determines whether a rising continental power can project its "hard power" into the global commons or remains bottled up behind its own coastlines.
**Node Applicability:** Global Systems and Strategic Competition. Directly relevant to `china-taiwan-indopacific` and `global-trade` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Is the actor attempting to transition from a "territorial" mindset of coastal defense to a "blue-water" force capable of securing sea lines of communication to distant markets?
**Connection Implication:** Weight maritime chokepoint nodes as "fulcrum constraints" for the entire energy and manufacturing viability of Northeast Asian economies; instability here acts as systemic contagion across `global-energy` and `global-trade` simultaneously.
**Red Team Challenge:** The proliferation of land-based pipelines and transcontinental railroads (e.g., Central Asian routes) may eventually lower the strategic weight of these maritime bottlenecks.

**Framework Name:** The Reverse Great Wall (Kaplan / Holmes and Yoshihara)
**Core Idea:** Physical geography -- specifically the "First Island Chain" stretching from Japan to Indonesia -- forms a natural barrier that can be utilized as a "Great Wall in reverse" to box in a continental navy. Control of these "guard towers," particularly Taiwan as the central and tallest tower, dictates whether a naval power can "radiate" influence across the Pacific or is denied access by land-based asymmetric systems.
**Node Applicability:** Strategic Competition and Kinetic Conflict. Primarily applies to `china-taiwan-indopacific`.
**Key Analytical Question:** Do military signals indicate a shifting "access-denial" capability that effectively expands a defensive "bubble" over contested waters?
**Connection Implication:** Heighten the correlation between `china-taiwan-indopacific` and `global-systems` nodes; the collapse or absorption of Taiwan fundamentally breaks the existing maritime security order.
**Red Team Challenge:** Disruptive technologies such as overlapping missile ranges and space-based surveillance may allow powers to project force over the island chain without requiring physical territorial control.

**Framework Name:** Chokepoint Asymmetry and Dissuasion (Kaplan / Mahan)
**Core Idea:** Maritime stability depends on a global enforcer ensuring freedom of the seas, yet this is increasingly threatened by the "territorialization" of narrow waterways. In specific chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz, a weaker regional power can use intricate coastlines, bays, and islands to hide asymmetric assets that "dissuade" a superior navy through the threat of unacceptable harm.
**Node Applicability:** Global Systems and Kinetic Conflict. Critical for `iran-hormuz`, `yemen-red-sea`, and `global-energy` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Does the signal indicate a regional power seeking "battle fleet supremacy" (Mahanian) or merely the "asymmetric" capability to deny others use of the chokepoint?
**Connection Implication:** Treat localized kinetic signals in chokepoint nodes as leading indicators for systemic contagion -- a local event in `iran-hormuz` resonates immediately across `global-energy` and `global-trade`.
**Red Team Challenge:** A transition toward a concert of powers or a "fleet in being" among multiple naval allies may create enough system redundancy to mitigate the loss of any single chokepoint.

## II. Great Power Competition, Polarity Shifts, and Structural Rivalry

**Framework Name:** The External Order Cycle (Dalio)
**Core Idea:** International orders shift when a rising challenger reaches a comparable position of strength to the reigning power, leading to five types of "war": trade, technology, capital, geopolitical, and military. The conflict realigns the world order to match the new reality of wealth and power.
**Node Applicability:** Strategic Competition. Primary focus on `china-domestic` and `us-domestic` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Do the current signals across trade, tech, and capital indicate a "test of power" that the existing order is no longer able to suppress?
**Connection Implication:** High correlation between internal economic health (debt/cohesion) and the ability to project external strength; weight internal signals as leading indicators of external conflict.
**Red Team Challenge:** The "diversifying effect" of a multipolar world may make the global system smoother and less volatile than the decline of any single hegemon suggests.

---

**Framework Name:** The Thucydidean Trap / Relative Gains (Papic / Kaplan)
**Core Idea:** Rival states in a structural rivalry prioritize "relative gains" (how much they gain vs. their opponent) over "absolute gains," leading to a zero-sum prisoner's dilemma where cooperation is avoided to prevent an opponent from gaining future military surplus.
**Node Applicability:** Strategic Competition. Relevant to `china-taiwan-indopacific` and `global-trade` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Is the state's policy designed to maximize its own economy or to disproportionately damage the rival's long-term growth?
**Connection Implication:** Weight trade and technology signals as security signals; in this framework, there is no distinction between "commercial" and "military" advantage.
**Red Team Challenge:** Multipolarity allows third-party players (EU, India, Brazil) to moderate economic constraints, preventing full bipolar bifurcation.

## III. Long-Cycle Decline, Internal Cohesion, and Regime Fragility

**Framework Name:** The Big Cycle of Internal Order (Dalio)
**Core Idea:** Nations transition through six stages, from "New Order" (Stage 1) to "Peace and Prosperity" (Stage 3) to "Civil War/Revolution" (Stage 6). The transition is driven by a "Classic Toxic Mix": high debt burdens, large wealth/values gaps, and a severe negative economic shock.
**Node Applicability:** Global Systems and Strategic Competition. Currently applies to `us-domestic` (Stage 5) and `china-domestic` (approaching Stage 4/5).
**Key Analytical Question:** Is the node exhibiting signs of Stage 5 -- specifically bankrupt government finances combined with big wealth gaps?
**Connection Implication:** Internal disorder signals (e.g., rules being disregarded, emotional attacks on institutions) should be weighted as high-probability indicators of a coming shift in the node's external behavior.
**Red Team Challenge:** Democratic systems "bend" more than autocracies, allowing for peaceful leadership changes that can delay or reverse the progression toward Stage 6.

---

**Framework Name:** The Median Voter Theorem as Constraint (Papic)
**Core Idea:** In the long term, policymakers are constrained by the position of the "median voter" (or the "median citizen" in autocracies like China). Survival requires asymptotically approaching the median position on the central issue of the day, regardless of leader preference.
**Node Applicability:** Global Systems and Regional Pivots. Relevant to `us-domestic`, `latin-america`, and `china-domestic` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Has the "median voter" moved toward radicalism or dirigisme, and is the leadership being forced to mirror that shift to maintain power?
**Connection Implication:** Use voter and public sentiment data as a "fulcrum constraint"; if the median moves, the entire policy trajectory of the node must be re-baselined.
**Red Team Challenge:** Mass irrationality (fear from pandemics or terrorism) can temporarily allow outliers to dominate the discussion, delaying constraint-based outcomes.

## IV. Deglobalization, Supply Chain Vulnerability, and Resource Competition

**Framework Name:** Decivilization via Supply Chain Collapse (Zeihan)
**Core Idea:** The American-led Order made geography matter less by institutionalizing global security, allowing for labor hyperspecialization. As the US withdraws, the world transitions from "cheap/better/faster" to "pricier/worse/slower," leading to a cascade of reinforcing breakdowns in energy and food systems.
**Node Applicability:** Global Systems and Kinetic Conflict. High vulnerability for extractive economies and megacities dependent on long-haul shipping. Directly feeds `global-trade`, `global-energy`, and `sahel-west-africa` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Does this node possess the diversity, reach, and redundancy to sustain itself without global trade?
**Connection Implication:** Weight signals of near-shoring or desourcing as survivability markers; weight signals of maritime instability in the First Island Chain as catastrophic for global manufacturing.
**Red Team Challenge:** Human desperation in a "dark world" often leads to breakthroughs in labor productivity and materials science (e.g., Post-Black Death effect).

---

**Framework Name:** Terminal Demographics (Zeihan / Friedman)
**Core Idea:** The global worker and consumer base is aging into mass retirement without a replacement generation, ending the 500-year history of "more." Countries with terminal demographics (China, Germany, Italy) face complete demographic collapse within a single generation.
**Node Applicability:** Global Systems. Applies to all advanced industrial countries, with the US as a relative exception. Most directly relevant to `china-domestic` and `russia-domestic` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Has the node passed the point of no return where there are more retirees than young adults?
**Connection Implication:** Weight demographic signals as the ultimate structural anchor; regardless of policy, a node with a collapsing worker cadre cannot maintain an industrial base or fund a global military.
**Red Team Challenge:** Automation and robotics may substitute for human labor faster than the demographic bust accelerates.

## V. Escalation Dynamics, Deterrence Thresholds, and Conflict Triggers

**Framework Name:** Two-Level Game Theory (Papic / Putnam)
**Core Idea:** International negotiations are constrained by domestic "win-sets" -- the set of agreements that would be accepted by domestic interest groups. Policymakers cannot conclude foreign agreements that fall outside these domestic win-sets without losing their political capital.
**Node Applicability:** Strategic Competition and Kinetic Conflict. Relevant to `iran-hormuz`, `ukraine-russia`, and `us-domestic` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** What is the domestic "win-set" for this leader, and does the proposed international deal fall outside of it?
**Connection Implication:** Weight domestic political capital signals (e.g., approval ratings, legislative math) as leading indicators of a node's flexibility in international crises.
**Red Team Challenge:** Leaders may engage in "hold-me-back" moments -- using aggressive rhetoric to satisfy domestic audiences while having no intention of kinetic follow-through.

---

**Framework Name:** The J-Curve of Structural Reform (Papic)
**Core Idea:** Painful structural reforms drain political capital, pushing a government into a "Danger Zone" where the populace may reject the policy before its long-term benefits materialize. Ample political capital is a material constraint on whether a state can afford to pursue unpopular but necessary survival strategies.
**Node Applicability:** Regional Pivots. Relevant to `south-asia`, `latin-america`, and `gcc-gulf` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Is the leader currently in the Danger Zone of a reform cycle, making them more vulnerable to populism or external coercion?
**Connection Implication:** Weight signals of domestic economic protest as indicators of reduced external risk tolerance.
**Red Team Challenge:** In autocracies, middle-class support can act as a buffer, allowing the regime to stay in the Danger Zone longer than a democracy.

## VI. Economic Coercion, Financial Signaling, and Market-as-Intelligence

**Framework Name:** The Constraint Framework (Papic)
**Core Idea:** Policy outcomes are driven by material constraints (political, economic, financial, geopolitical), not leader preferences. Preferences are optional; constraints are not. Analysts should study the constraints, not the Prince.
**Node Applicability:** Global Systems and Strategic Competition. Universal applicability across all 18 nodes as a meta-framework.
**Key Analytical Question:** What is the "fulcrum constraint" -- the single barrier that bends all other factors -- in this signal?
**Connection Implication:** Weight diagnostic data (observable, material facts like bond yields or geographic barriers) higher than narrative data (rhetoric, speeches, preferences).
**Red Team Challenge:** In a crisis, policymakers often break or rewrite hybrid constraints like laws and constitutions if they have the political capital to do so.

---

**Framework Name:** The Capital Market Cycle / Debt Supercycle (Dalio / Papic)
**Core Idea:** Long-term debt cycles (50-100 years) culminate in "Monetary Policy 3" -- where central banks monetize massive government deficits because interest rate cuts and QE are no longer effective. This era is characterized by dirigisme (state-directed economics) and the end of laissez-faire.
**Node Applicability:** Global Systems. Applies to `global-financial`, `us-domestic`, and `china-domestic` nodes.
**Key Analytical Question:** Is the node currently monetizing debt to fill financial holes while facing large wealth gaps?
**Connection Implication:** Weight signals of central bank independence lower; weight signals of fiscal and monetary coordination as markers of late-stage empire decline.
**Red Team Challenge:** The market and policymakers have a reflexive relationship; market perceptions can temporarily alter economic fundamentals, creating ibuprofen-like effects that delay the cycle.

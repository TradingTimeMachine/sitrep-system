The most analytically significant development of Week 3 is not the 36-hour lifespan of Project Freedom or the exchange of fire in the Strait of Hormuz on Monday. It is a phrase from China's Foreign Ministry announcing that Araghchi's visit to Beijing was arranged "yingyao," at Beijing's invitation. That single diplomatic term, buried in a routine ministry statement, marks the moment China moved from passive beneficiary of the Iran conflict to active architect of its resolution. Everything that follows from that activation will define the next phase of this crisis more than any ceasefire announcement, ultimatum, or naval operation.

Beijing invited Araghchi to Beijing ten days before Trump arrives for the May 14-15 summit. China's Foreign Minister Wang Yi called for Hormuz reopening "as soon as possible" while simultaneously endorsing Iran's right to peaceful nuclear energy and ordering Chinese companies to disregard U.S. sanctions on Iranian oil purchases. This is a three-track play: push Iran toward Hormuz flexibility, protect Iran's oil revenue from U.S. economic pressure, and position Beijing as the indispensable mediator at the summit. It is the most sophisticated diplomatic maneuver any actor has made since Pakistan brokered the original ceasefire on April 8. And it worked immediately: Araghchi signaled in Beijing that Hormuz reopening "can be resolved promptly," which is the strongest civilian track signal toward the decoupling deal since it was first proposed.

The IRGC's simultaneous behavior on Monday, attacking U.S. vessels and GCC infrastructure on the same day Project Freedom launched, reveals the dual-track thesis at its most operationally complete. While Araghchi was preparing to travel to Beijing to signal Hormuz flexibility, IRGC gunboats were attacking vessels in the strait and IRGC missiles were hitting a UAE oil facility. The civilian track signals availability. The military track tests the operational commitment behind U.S. announcements. Both tracks serve the same IRGC strategic goal: determine whether Project Freedom was a genuine military commitment or a diplomatic gesture, and shape the negotiating environment before the summit. The answer the IRGC received was unambiguous. Trump paused Project Freedom within 36 hours, citing "great progress," after Iran applied military pressure. This is the fifth iteration of the deadline-and-retreat pattern that the IRGC has been calibrating since March.

Rubio's declaration that Operation Epic Fury is definitively over and the focus is now Hormuz reopening represents a public reframing of U.S. objectives that has not been fully absorbed in analytical commentary. The United States entered this conflict with stated goals of regime change and nuclear dismantlement. It is now publicly framing its primary objective as freedom of navigation through an international waterway. This is not simply a change in tactics. It is a change in what the U.S. is willing to settle for. If the Trump-Xi summit produces a Hormuz-for-sanctions-relief arrangement, the war that began on February 28 will have achieved none of its original stated objectives while allowing the IRGC to reconstitute its military capability, harden its Hormuz permission regime as a precedent, and defer nuclear constraints indefinitely. The IRGC will have won the strategic argument while losing the military exchange.

The economic timeline is now compressing faster than the diplomatic track is moving. Chevron's CEO warned this week that physical shortages will begin in Asia within weeks, move to Europe, and eventually hit the United States. The IMF has warned of global recession risk. U.S. gas prices average $4.30 per gallon. The Iranian civilian population is experiencing minimum wage collapse, unpaid wages, and food price inflation. Both sides are accumulating costs that make resolution more urgent even as the structural incompatibility on nuclear constraints remains unchanged. This creates a perverse incentive: both sides have reason to accept a Hormuz-for-sanctions-relief deal even though it resolves the tactical problem while leaving the structural problem intact, and both sides know it.

What happens at the Trump-Xi summit on May 14-15 will determine the next 90 days. If China brokers a Hormuz deal, it will be a tactical resolution that defers the nuclear question to a renegotiation under materially worse conditions, with Iran more reconstituted, China more economically exposed to any future disruption, and the U.S. having publicly retreated from its original war objectives. If China and the U.S. cannot bridge the nuclear gap at the summit level, the ceasefire continues degrading under its own contradictions until a triggering event, in Lebanon, in Hormuz, or in the South China Sea, collapses it. The IRGC is the only actor in this system whose preferred outcome improves with every week that passes. That arithmetic has not changed. What has changed is that China, the one actor with genuine economic leverage over Tehran, has finally decided to enter the room.

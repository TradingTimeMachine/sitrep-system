## INTELLIGENCE BRIEF — ISSUE 005
*For publication and distribution.*

---

Donald Trump arrived in Beijing this morning carrying the most consequential foreign policy problem of his second term and the weakest negotiating hand of any U.S. president visiting China in recent memory. The Iran war that began on February 28 is now ten weeks old. The Strait of Hormuz has been closed to normal commercial traffic for that entire period. Half a billion barrels of cumulative supply disruption have already been absorbed by the global economy. And the United States' primary ask of the summit, that China use its leverage as the buyer of over 80% of Iranian crude to push Tehran toward concessions, runs directly against China's structural interest in watching America exhaust itself in an unwinnable confrontation.

The Soufan Center's pre-summit analysis captures the strategic reality with unusual precision: Xi is unlikely to spend political capital helping Trump clean up a mess he created. Once the conflict winds down, Tehran will still be in control of the Strait of Hormuz, determining which countries receive safe passage and which pay for the privilege. China has already weathered the worst of the energy shock through its pre-loaded reserves. Its April export data showed resilience, with exports up 14% year on year, suggesting the war has not yet produced the domestic economic crisis that would force Beijing toward coercive Iran mediation. Xi enters this summit with leverage, patience, and no compelling domestic reason to deliver what Washington wants.

The week's most significant analytical development is not the summit's opening but what preceded it. Iran's formal counterproposal, delivered through Pakistan and deemed "totally unacceptable" by Trump, demanded formal U.S. recognition of Iranian sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz alongside war reparations and sanctions relief. This is categorically different from the earlier decoupling proposal that offered Hormuz reopening in exchange for blockade relief. Sovereignty recognition is a permanent legal status claim. It is asking the United States to formally endorse, in international law, Iranian ownership of a waterway through which 20% of global energy trade flows. The IRGC is not negotiating toward a deal. It is establishing the legal architecture for a permanent Hormuz permission regime and using the diplomatic track to test whether Washington can be maneuvered into accepting it.

The uranium reversal, where Iran made a verbal commitment to allow U.S. removal of enriched uranium and withdrew it before documentation, provides the clearest single-event confirmation of the mechanism this system has been tracking since Issue 001. Araghchi can signal concessions. The IRGC can reverse them before they become binding. This is not a dysfunctional negotiating team. It is a deliberately structured system in which the civilian track creates the appearance of diplomatic progress while the military track ensures nothing is actually committed. Trump correctly identified the Iranian leadership as "very disjointed." It is not disjointed. It is operating as designed.

The IRGC's expansion of kinetic operations to GCC states this week, attacking UAE infrastructure on May 4 and sending a paramilitary team to Kuwait on May 12, establishes a pattern that the summit's diplomatic momentum has temporarily suppressed but not resolved. The IRGC is signaling to the Gulf states that their geographic exposure to Iranian military action extends beyond Hormuz shipping. The UAE has absorbed two attacks without formal attribution. Kuwait has formally attributed the paramilitary infiltration. If this pattern continues after the summit concludes, GCC states will face the choice they have been deferring since April 13: formal military alignment with the United States or accommodation of Iranian regional dominance. That choice, if it forces a decision, would reshape the regional security architecture in ways that neither Washington nor Tehran has fully calculated.

The Houthi restraint in the Red Sea deserves its own analytical accounting. No commercial vessel has been struck in 2026. This is not incapacity. According to Cipher Brief reporting, the Houthis declined an Iranian request to attack Red Sea shipping in late March, choosing to target Israel instead. The IRGC is holding the Houthi card in reserve, and the Houthis are calculating independently that the activation moment has not arrived. This is a more sophisticated proxy management dynamic than the thesis initially assessed, and it means the dual-chokepoint scenario, Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb simultaneously active, remains available to the IRGC as an escalation option that has not yet been deployed. The summit outcome will determine whether that option is used or kept in reserve.

What happens in Beijing over the next 48 hours will not be fully visible in public communiques. The analytically relevant question is not what Trump and Xi say publicly about Iran but what China commits to privately regarding its financial system's support for Iranian oil purchases. If China's state banks quietly accelerate their withdrawal from sanctioned Iranian refiner lending, the economic pressure on the IRGC will intensify in ways that become observable within 30 days. If China makes no private commitment, the summit will have produced thin deliverables, the IRGC will read it as a strategic validation of its endurance approach, and the ceasefire will enter its most unstable phase since April 7 without any new pressure mechanism on either side. The IRGC is the only actor in this system whose preferred outcome improves with the passage of time, and it has now been waiting out five weeks of ceasefire while accumulating reconstitution progress, establishing a toll revenue stream in Hormuz, and watching the United States exhaust its diplomatic options one by one.

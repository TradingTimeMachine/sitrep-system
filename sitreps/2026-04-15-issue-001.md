Core Thesis
The Middle East is not experiencing a war with a clear arc toward resolution. It is experiencing a structural state failure in Iran that has been misread as a negotiating situation. The killing of Khamenei did not weaken the Islamic Republic's coercive capacity — it concentrated that capacity in the IRGC while removing the political architecture needed to make binding commitments. The United States is conducting blockade operations and peace negotiations simultaneously against an entity that cannot constitutionally authorize a deal and has every military incentive not to. Meanwhile, the conflict's greatest external beneficiary — Russia — has acquired a perverse interest in prolonging it, and the external actor with the most leverage over Iran — China — is unwilling to exercise that leverage publicly. The ceasefire is not a pause before peace. It is a reorganization interval before the next phase.

Regional Assessments
I. The Persian Axis — Iran, Iraq, Proxy Network
Status: Critical
Khamenei was assassinated on 28 February during joint US-Israeli strikes. Mojtaba Khamenei was installed as Supreme Leader on 9 March in an IRGC-orchestrated process — rushed through under wartime pressure, with reformist Assembly members coerced into compliance. Mojtaba has not appeared in public since his appointment.
Three IRGC veterans now hold operational state power: IRGC commander Ahmad Vahidi, newly installed SNSC Secretary Zolghadr (appointed against President Pezeshkian's wishes), and wartime adviser Mohsen Reza'i. The constitutional mechanism requiring Supreme Leader confirmation of SNSC decisions is structurally inoperative. Iran is being run by a military council in the name of a leader who cannot be seen.
Nuclear infrastructure: Satellite imagery confirms Iran is actively clearing debris from underground "missile city" entrances during the ceasefire window. These facilities were designed to absorb a first strike and reconstitute. The IRGC has no institutional incentive to halt reconstitution and no figure with sufficient authority is ordering them to. The ceasefire is functioning as a reconstitution interval.
Proxy network: Decapitation of Iran's senior leadership has degraded centralized proxy command. Hezbollah, Houthis, and Iraqi militia factions retain operational capacity but are operating with longer decision loops and more autonomous local commanders.

II. Strait of Hormuz — The Chokepoint as Battlefield
Status: Critical
Iran closed the strait on 28 February, halting approximately 10 million barrels per day. A ceasefire was declared 8 April but Iran has not reopened the strait. The ADNOC CEO confirmed on 9 April that the strait remained closed despite the ceasefire because Iran is "restricting and conditioning traffic." 230 loaded oil tankers are waiting inside the Gulf.
Complication: Iran reportedly lost track of mines it planted, making full reopening physically impossible regardless of political will. On 13 April, the US declared a naval blockade of Iranian ports. US Navy destroyers began mine clearance operations, which Iran denounced as a ceasefire violation.
The mine problem as strategic variable: Whether Iran genuinely lost control of its mines or is using this as a compliance excuse with built-in deniability is the key analytical question. Either way the result is the same: the strait stays functionally closed, Iran maintains leverage, and the US blockade becomes the visible cause of continued disruption.
Alternative routing: Saudi East-West Pipeline and Aramco Abqaiq facility offer partial bypass (~5M bbl/day). Cannot replace full Hormuz throughput but prevents complete supply collapse for GCC producers.
Watch:
First tanker transiting Hormuz with US Navy escort
Any attack on Aramco Abqaiq — catastrophic escalation signal

III. The Levant — Israel, Lebanon, West Bank, Syria
Status: Critical
Israel is simultaneously managing the Iran ceasefire, an active ground invasion of southern Lebanon (five divisions deployed including 98th Division added 7 April), ongoing West Bank operations, partial Gaza deployment, and a growing footprint in southern Syria.
Finance Minister Smotrich's 9 April speech explicitly mapping territorial expansion into Lebanon to the Litani River, into the West Bank, and into Syrian buffer zones represents the first official articulation of what analysts have been calling the "Greater Israel" consolidation strategy.
Lebanon: On 8 April, Israel killed over 300 people in a 10-minute bombardment — the deadliest single day of the conflict. Direct Israel-Lebanon diplomatic talks announced 9 April, began 15 April — first in decades. However, Hezbollah's leader rejected the talks wholesale and fighting continued alongside the diplomatic opening. Israel is negotiating with the Lebanese government while destroying Lebanese territory — a contradiction Beirut cannot sustain indefinitely.
West Bank — the quiet escalation: OCHA data shows Palestinian settler attack injuries rising from 30/month in 2024 to 105/month in 2026. Over 1,697 Palestinians displaced — surpassing all of 2025. Israeli government measures actively accelerating settlement expansion. This is systematic territorial consolidation occurring under cover of the larger regional war.
Syria: US withdrew from strategic northern Syria base in February. Israel preventing Iraqi militia crossings through Syrian territory.

V. Red Sea / Yemen — Houthis, STC, Southern Fracture
Status: Elevated

Saudi recalibrating — sidelining UAE-aligned figures. North remains functional Houthi proto-state. Pakistan working on Lebanon and Yemen ceasefires alongside Iran track.

Global Power Inputs
China — Maximum Structural Exposure
38% of Hormuz oil flow is China-bound. Half of China's seaborne oil supply. Iranian crude is 13% of Chinese imports. China pre-loaded reserves aggressively, but March imports already falling. China allowed Chinese-flagged ships preferential Hormuz passage under Iranian permission — a de facto two-tier strait regime. Beijing condemned the US blockade as "dangerous and irresponsible." Xi met Lavrov on 15 April — same day blockade took effect. China has maximum incentive to end this quickly but refuses to be seen doing Washington's bidding publicly. Signal: pressure building but not yet converting to action.
Russia — Strategic Beneficiary With Perverse Incentive
Russia's energy revenues were in freefall before 28 February — below $10B/month, Kremlin preparing 10% spending cuts, Western sanctions finally working. The Iran war reversed all of that overnight. Urals crude hit $116/barrel on 2 April — nearly double Russia's budget assumption. KSE Institute estimates $45B–$151B additional 2026 revenues. 90% of Russia's crude exports now go to China and India. Putin gains fiscal relief, Ukraine war funding, European anxiety leverage, and Ukraine negotiation pressure. Russia has every incentive to see this conflict extend. This is the most underappreciated dynamic in the entire situation.
Pakistan — Sole Active Mediator, Overextended
Pakistan brokered the 8 April ceasefire and hosted the Islamabad talks that collapsed 12 April. Vance left with a "final and best offer." Now working on Lebanon and Yemen ceasefires simultaneously. Unique access to all parties without being perceived as US proxy. But cannot force a deal Iran's actual power structure doesn't want. Indispensable but limited.
Europe / Global South — Collateral Damage Accumulating
ECB warning stagflation and recession in Germany and Italy. OECD projects G20 inflation at 4% for 2026. EU defence commissioner: US missile stocks overstretched, affecting Ukraine aid. Brazil condemned Israel's Lebanon invasion. Non-Western normative opposition accumulating in ways that will constrain whatever regional architecture emerges.

Red Team Challenge
The IRGC-capture thesis may overstate institutional coherence. 31 decentralized provincial corps with second-tier commanders suggests possible fragmentation, not unified hardline control.
Mojtaba's silence may be a security decision, not incapacitation. His written statement showed a leader articulating both defiance and diplomatic openings — not puppet language.
China's public condemnation alongside private pragmatism may be the most important underplayed dynamic. The Xi-Lavrov meeting on 15 April may be about calibrating exactly how much pressure to apply on Tehran and when.
The Russia windfall analysis may be overstated as a strategic factor. Russian inflation at 5.9%, interest rates at 15%. The windfall buys time — it does not restructure Russia's underlying economic fragility.

Final Intelligence Brief
The central misreading of the current Middle East situation is that it is a war approaching resolution. It is not. It is a war that has completed its first military phase and entered a more dangerous second phase — one characterized not by kinetic exchange but by institutional vacuum, competing blockades, and a structural mismatch between what the United States needs from Iran and what Iran's actual power holders can give.
The killing of Khamenei did not hollow out the Islamic Republic. It concentrated its coercive capacity in the IRGC while simultaneously destroying the political mechanisms needed to make binding diplomatic commitments. Mojtaba Khamenei, installed under IRGC pressure and unseen since his appointment, cannot constitutionally authorize the SNSC decisions that any real agreement requires. The United States is negotiating with an entity that is simultaneously conducting mine clearance evasion, missile base reconstitution, and ceasefire-compliant posturing — because the people running Iran have no institutional reason to stop doing any of those things and no figure above them with the authority to order otherwise.
The Hormuz crisis has metastasized from a military action into a permanent structural disruption with its own logic. Iran's mine-laying may have been more reckless than strategic — reports that Iran itself lost track of the mines suggest a military decision made without full operational control. The result is a chokepoint that neither side can fully open. The US blockade enforces what Iran cannot undo. Two hundred and thirty loaded tankers sit waiting inside the Gulf. The IEA's 400 million barrel reserve release is the buffer keeping global energy markets from a full shock event — but it is a finite buffer, not a solution.
The global power inputs make resolution harder, not easier. Russia's windfall — potentially $45 to $151 billion in additional 2026 revenues — gives Putin every incentive to see the conflict extend. Every week of high oil prices funds the Ukraine campaign that US attention is too stretched to adequately counter. China, which absorbs 38% of normal Hormuz flow, has the greatest leverage over Tehran but the least willingness to use it publicly. Pakistan, the only party with access to all sides, has demonstrated its indispensability and its limits in the same week.
Meanwhile, in the shadow of the larger conflict, Israel is conducting a systematic territorial consolidation that is remaking the physical facts of the Levant. The Lebanon invasion, the West Bank acceleration, the Syria buffer zone, the Smotrich speech mapping a Greater Israel political architecture — these are not peripheral to the Iran crisis. They are its downstream consequences, proceeding under cover of the world's attention being elsewhere.
The reason all of this matters beyond the region is that the postwar Middle East — whatever form it takes — will have rewritten the assumptions of energy security, great power competition, and American military primacy that have governed global order since 1991. What is being settled in the Strait of Hormuz is not just an Iran question. It is a question about who controls the world's most critical energy corridor, on what terms, and whether the institutional frameworks that have managed that question for four decades are still functional. The answer so far is that they are not.
